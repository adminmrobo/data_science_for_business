Finding the One That Doesn't Fit

Out of tens of thousands of payments, vouchers, and sensor records, how do we pick the "few odd ones" that people should look at first?

Anomaly detection means finding rare records that move differently from most of the data. This lecture runs through the five tools in a one-page infographic in turn: the Z-score, which looks at a single column; the Mahalanobis distance, which also looks at relationships between columns; the Isolation Forest, which cuts at random and isolates points; the autoencoder, which learns usual patterns and reconstructs them; and the threshold, which splits scores into "check" and "let pass."

The Doctor's thought cloud

"Odd" comes in kinds: sticking out alone, mismatched pairs, belonging nowhere, a different shape. Each kind has its own ruler. And after measuring, you have to decide "from what score to start looking" (box ⑤).

Why anomaly detection?

1. Anomalies are rare and there is no answer key

Fraudulent payments and wrongly entered vouchers make up about 1% of the total, and cases where anomalies are marked in advance are rare. So we can't learn from answers as in the classification of week 7, and instead use a method that measures "how different from usual."[4]

2. We cannot check everything

The number of cases an audit team can check in a day is limited. The real use of anomaly detection is ranking "which to look at first."

3. A missed case is costly

For card misuse, equipment signals just before failure, or network intrusion, missing one case is costly. Conversely, too many alarms make people ignore them.[4]

First, a myth to drop: "96% accuracy means a good detector"

Of the 574 payments in box ⑤, 24 (about 4%) are anomalies. A detector that suspects nothing and just answers "all normal" still scores an accuracy of 550 ÷ 574 ≈ 95.8%. Yet it catches not a single anomaly. When anomalies are rare, look not at accuracy but at "how many of the true anomalies did it catch (recall)" and "how many of the alarms were real (precision)" (Week 7).

Big picture: make scores, then split them by threshold

① Z-score: one column→② Mahalanobis: relationships between columns→③ Isolation Forest: several clusters→④ Reconstruction error: patterns across many columns→⑤ Threshold: score to decision
In everyday terms

It's like an accountant flipping through a pile of receipts and setting aside one that looks odd. The accountant holds a picture of "usual" in mind and pulls out whatever differs from it.

In data science

①–④ are all different ways to compute an anomaly score (how different from usual) for each record. A score alone decides nothing; only records passing the threshold in ⑤ become "to check." The core summary in the infographic follows the same structure.[1]

1One column

Z-score: how many standard deviations from the mean

z = (x − μ) ÷ σ, anomaly if |z| > τData: 200 meal payment records (example, 3 anomalies hidden)

FirstWhy use this method

When looking at a single number like meal, business-trip, or order amounts, we want to measure "how much does this value spike from usual" on the same scale. Amounts vary in spread, so the Z-score measures against the spread of that data as the ruler.

Simply put: whether 180 cm counts as "tall" depends on how varied the heights in the class are. Measuring by how many hand-spans (standard deviations) it sits from the average gives the same standard in any class.

In statistics

z = (x − μ) ÷ σ. If the data is close to a bell shape (normal distribution), values with |z| > 3 are only about 3 in 1,000, so they're treated as anomaly candidates. In the bell-shaped graphic of the infographic, the red tails at both ends are this region.[1]

The Doctor's thought cloud

Far from the mean means suspicious. But if one huge value drags the "mean" and the "spread" along, the ruler stretches and other anomalies hide. In that case, measure with the median.

Explanation

  1. With "mean and standard deviation" and τ = 3, only one case, the 480,000 won payment, is flagged. The 118,000 and 96,000 won payments look odd even to the eye, yet they pass.
  2. The reason is that the one 480,000 won case inflated the standard deviation. Without it, the standard deviation is about 13,000 won; with it, 33,000 won. This phenomenon, in which one large outlier hides the others, is called the masking effect.
  3. Switching to "median and MAD" measures the center as the median and the spread as "the median of distances from the median × 1.4826." Not swayed by one or two extremes, all three cases surface.

Limit of this step: the Z-score looks at one column at a time. A case like "a one-day business trip with a 1,080,000 won allowance" looks ordinary column by column, but is odd when viewed together, and the Z-score cannot catch it. → ② Mahalanobis distance

Calculation method

z = (x − mean) ÷ standard deviation · robust z = (x − median) ÷ (1.4826 × MAD), where MAD = median(|x − median|)

1.4826 is a constant that puts the MAD on the same scale as the standard deviation when the data are normal.

Taking it to the field

In audit and finance settings, scores are not used alone but together with business rules. Rules such as "weekend or late-night payments," "the same amount repeated at the same merchant," or "payments split just under the approval limit" have clear reasons and can be checked right away. Scores serve as a net for what the rules did not foresee, and each alarm carries the reason "which column differed from usual, and by how much" (for example, meal z = 5.8) when passed to the checker.

Limit: rules catch only known schemes, and scores explain their reasons weakly (especially ③ and ④). Using both fills each other's blind spots.

2Relationship

Mahalanobis distance: a point that breaks a joint-movement relationship

D² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ), anomaly if D² > τData: 150 business trips (days · allowance, example)

FirstWhy use this method

Two columns that move together, such as business-trip days and allowance, can look ordinary when viewed separately but be odd in combination. The Mahalanobis distance measures distance reflecting the relationship between the two columns.

Simply put: among points spread in a rugby-ball shape, a point far out along the ball's long axis is "possible," while a point sticking out slightly along the short axis is "odd." It is like using a tape measure fitted to the shape of the ball.

assistant

Symbols like Σ⁻¹ are too hard. What does it actually do?

In school math it's the distance on a coordinate plane (Pythagoras), re-measured after stretching and shrinking along the directions in which the data spread.

x − μ
The direction and amount this point deviates from the mean (a two-column arrow)
Σ (sigma)
Covariance matrix: a table collecting each column's spread and how much two columns move together
Σ⁻¹
The inverse of that table: a scale that gives small weight to directions of large spread and large weight to directions of small spread
D²
Result. With one column, it equals z²
Try it with numbers

If two columns are unrelated (covariance 0) and z₁ = 2, z₂ = 2, then D² = 2² + 2² = 8, D ≈ 2.8. In data with a strong relationship, if one column moves +2 and the other −2 ("in opposite directions"), D becomes much larger than this.

assistant

If the data spread diagonally, the tape is laid diagonally too. Now I see why a one-day trip with 1,080,000 won looks so far off.

In everyday terms

Longer trips have larger allowances. One-day trips are common, and 1,080,000 won allowances are common, but "one day, 1,080,000 won" is odd. Measuring the two numbers separately won't reveal it; you have to measure along the "direction they travel together."

In statistics

The Mahalanobis distance uses the mean vector μ and the covariance matrix Σ to re-measure distance along the directions in which the data spread (the ellipse). Along the long axis of the ellipse, even far points come out close; along the short axis, even a slight deviation comes out far.[1][3]

Explanation

  1. The square box is the boundary from Z-scoring days and allowance separately. All three hidden cases are inside the box and pass; instead, the 7-day trip, whose relationship is consistent, falls outside the box and raises an alarm.
  2. The tilted ellipse is the boundary where the Mahalanobis distance D equals the threshold. The three hidden cases have D of 4.8 to 6.8, far outside the ellipse, while the 7-day trip stays near the boundary with D ≈ 3.3.
  3. With two columns and data close to normal, D² follows a chi-square distribution with 2 degrees of freedom, so ordinary records with D² > 9 (D > 3) are about 1.1%.

Limit of this step: the Mahalanobis distance treats the data as "one ellipse centered on one mean." If there are two or more clusters, such as customers who spend small amounts often and those who spend large amounts occasionally, the empty space between the clusters lies near the mean and is measured as close. → ③ Isolation Forest

Calculation method

D² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ) · for two columns, D² = [a²·s₂₂ − 2ab·s₁₂ + b²·s₁₁] ÷ (s₁₁s₂₂ − s₁₂²), where a = x₁ − μ₁, b = x₂ − μ₂

If the covariance is 0 (the two columns do not move together), D² = z₁² + z₂², which equals the sum of the two Z-scores.

The masking effect of ① also occurs here. If the mean vector and covariance are computed including odd records, the ellipse can swell toward the anomaly. In practice, methods that estimate covariance robustly against outliers (such as MinCovDet and EllipticEnvelope in scikit-learn) are also used. This step used the simple mean and covariance.

3Isolate

Isolation Forest: how many cuts until a point is alone?

s(x) = 2^(−E[h(x)] ÷ c(n)), closer to 1 means more anomalousData: 292 payments (payment amount · daily payment count, two clusters, example)

FirstWhy this model

If customers split into "small amounts, often" and "large amounts, occasionally," the mean falls in the empty space between the two clusters. ①② measure against the mean and miss anomalies in that empty space. So we use the Isolation Forest, which measures not by distance but by how easily a point is separated.

Simply put: in a game of twenty questions, a person blending into a crowd takes many questions to pin down, while a person alone in a remote place is found in two or three. The fewer the questions, the more odd the point.

assistant

2 to a negative power... why does the score look like this?

In school math it's powers of 2 (exponents) and averages. Splitting 256 cases exactly in half takes 8 steps to isolate one (2⁸ = 256), but random cuts don't split evenly, so the average path is a little longer, around 10.

h(x)
How many cuts in one tree until this point is left alone
E[h(x)]
Average over 100 trees
c(n)
Average path length of a "typical point" in a tree built from n cases (about 10.2 when n = 256)
s(x)
2^(−E[h]/c). Shorter means closer to 1; average means 0.5
Try it with numbers

If the average path equals c(n), namely 10.2, then s = 2^(−1) = 0.5 (ordinary). If it is isolated in 3 steps, s = 2^(−3/10.2) ≈ 0.82, a candidate anomaly.

assistant

The faster it is separated, the closer to 1; ordinary points sit near 0.5! Since we don't measure distance, it doesn't matter that there are two clusters.

In everyday terms

In twenty questions, an ordinary person blending into the crowd needs many questions, while someone standing apart is found in two or three. The Isolation Forest, instead of questions, splits the data in half with random lines such as "is the amount under 60,000 won?" and counts how many steps it takes to be left alone.

In machine learning

Build several trees that split by randomly choosing a feature and a split position, and average the number of steps h(x) it takes for a point to be left alone. Isolated, far-off points have short average paths. Because it assumes no distance or distribution shape, it works even with several clusters.[1][2]

The white rings (top 12 by score) nearly skip the red dots in the empty space and attach to the outer points. On the Isolation Forest background, the white rings attach to many of the red dots in the empty space.

Limit of this step: the Isolation Forest cuts each column separately. For data with many columns, such as 24-hour visitor counts, where the "shape formed by the columns" matters, a day whose values at each hour are ordinary but whose overall shape is odd is not well isolated. → ④ Reconstruction error

Calculation method

c(n) = 2H(n − 1) − 2(n − 1) ÷ n, H(i) ≈ ln(i) + 0.5772 · s(x, n) = 2^(−E[h(x)] ÷ c(n))

The tree stops at depth log₂(256) = 8. For points not yet isolated by then, the path length is filled in by adding c(m) for the remaining m points (the original paper's method).[2] The h = 2 and h = 3 in the infographic's tree drawing are simplified drawings without this addition.

Taking it to the field

In detecting fraudulent card or online-shop payments, rather than blocking high-score payments right away, steps are set up in the order "request extra verification → agent check." This is because false alarms that block legitimate customers become customer complaints. Accumulating the confirmation results (was it really fraud?) lets you set the threshold in ⑤ and later move to a classification model that learns from answers (week 7).

Limit: fraud methods keep changing. A forest built from last month's data may fail to flag this month's new schemes, so it must be rebuilt periodically and the alarm rate monitored.

4Reconstruct

Reconstruction error: a day that cannot be redrawn from usual patterns

e(x) = ‖x − x̂‖² = Σ (xᵢ − x̂ᵢ)², anomaly if e(x) > τData: store visitors by hour, 146 days × 24 columns (example, 6 anomalous days hidden)

FirstWhy this model

Data like 24-hour visitor counts or dozens of equipment sensors, where there are many columns and the "shape" matters, look ordinary column by column. The autoencoder's reconstruction error learns the usual shape and finds the days that cannot be redrawn in that shape.

Simply put: condense a long meeting transcript into three summary points, then rewrite the transcript from only those points. Ordinary meetings get rewritten similarly, but a meeting totally unlike usual doesn't fit the points, so the rewritten text doesn't match.

If a three-column record (10, 20, 30) is reconstructed as (12, 18, 30), then e = (10 − 12)² + (20 − 18)² + (30 − 30)² = 4 + 4 + 0 = 8.

assistant

So a narrow bottleneck keeps only the usual shape. And if it is too wide, it just copies and finds nothing.

In everyday terms

Suppose we summarize a daily visitor graph with short notes such as "weekday type plus a few, weekend type plus a few," then redraw the graph from only the notes. On ordinary days it is redrawn almost identically, but on a day the store suddenly closed in the evening, the notes cannot express it, so the redrawn graph doesn't match.

In deep learning

An autoencoder is a neural network made of a part that compresses the input x into a narrow bottleneck z (the encoder f) and a part that expands z back into x̂ (the decoder g). Trained only on normal records, it reconstructs usual patterns well, and for never-seen patterns the reconstruction error e(x) becomes large.[1]

About the autoencoder in this step

The autoencoder in this step is a linear autoencoder with no curved (nonlinear) functions in its layers. It is known that training a linear autoencoder with squared error learns the same space as the axes that principal component analysis (PCA) finds[5], so here the principal components were computed directly from the covariance of the 140 normal days. Bottleneck k = k principal components.

Real-world autoencoders stack several curved layers and learn complex patterns that cannot be summarized linearly. The cost is longer training and trial and error to choose the bottleneck and the number of layers.

In generative AI terms

The idea that "what a model learned from normal data fails to explain is odd" carries over to generative AI as well. One example is using how "surprising" (perplexity) a language model finds a sentence as an anomaly signal in system logs or documents.

Limit of the analogy: a language model's "surprise" score also swings with sentence length, tone, and technical terms, so to use it directly as an anomaly score, the threshold must be set separately with validation data, as in ⑤.

5Decide

Threshold: the line that turns scores into "to check"

Anomaly if s(x) ≥ τ, normal if s(x) < τData: 574 payments = validation 287 + test 287 (example, 12 anomalies each)

FirstWhy use this method

①–④ only give scores. Considering how many cases the audit team can look at in a day and the loss from missing one, you must draw the line for "from what score on to check" to make it a working task.

Simply put: drawing a passing mark. Raise the line and those who pass are certain, but some who narrowly miss are lost; lower it and the reverse happens.

In data science

The infographic lists three common methods: flag the top few percent of scores (e.g., 1 to 5%) as anomalies, flag scores above mean + kσ (e.g., k = 3), or on validation data with known answers, take the point where a metric such as F1 is best as the threshold.[1] I added a fourth method that brings in business cost.

Explanation

Limit of this step: the ground truth in the validation data (were they truly anomalies?) comes from people checking. At first there are no answers, so you usually start with "top N%" and move to validation-based methods as confirmation results accumulate.

Taking it to the field — people who know the threshold slip under it

The moment a threshold becomes known, some people start dodging it. For example, once a rule like "transfers of 10 million won or more get checked" is public, people split transfers into several of 9.9 million won each, making every single record look ordinary. The z-score in box ①, which measures one record at a time, doesn't flag 9.9 million won. So on the shop floor, we turn features that look at several records together, such as "did the same person repeat amounts just under the threshold within a short time?", into new boxes, feed them into the score, and separately examine records clustered near the threshold.

Limits: There are also attacks that doctor the data to fool the detection model itself (adversarial data). How to filter out such attacks with statistical tests is covered, with experimental results, in Further reading [7].

Showdown: among payments in two clusters, who catches more with 15 alarms?

Summary: the five tools on one page

ToolWhat it measuresData it fitsLimit of this step
① Z-scoreHow many standard deviations a single column is from the meanOne column, one clusterCannot see relationships between columns; a large outlier masks others
② MahalanobisDistance measured along the relationship (ellipse)Several columns, one cluster, columns that move togetherWith several clusters, the empty space between them is measured as close
③ Isolation ForestNumber of random cuts until a point is separatedTable data, several clustersWeak on patterns across many columns where shape matters
④ Reconstruction errorDifference from redrawing with usual patternsMany-column patterns such as hourly, sensor, or image dataNeeds normal data for training; sensitive to bottleneck size
⑤ ThresholdScore → check / let passAll scoresBetter set once confirmation results (answers) have accumulated
Start simple

For one column, use the Z-score (median and MAD if possible); for several columns, start with the Mahalanobis distance and set a baseline. Use complex tools only when they beat the baseline.

Look at the data shape

Several clusters → Isolation Forest; patterns where time or sensors matter → autoencoder. Drawing a picture once is the first step in choosing a tool (week 12).

The business decides the threshold

Weigh the staff who can check, the loss when a case is missed, and the nuisance of false alarms to set the threshold, and keep refining it as confirmation results accumulate.

Three common myths

"Anomaly = wrongdoing"?

No. An anomaly just means "different from usual." Records that are long and expensive but legitimate, like the 7-day business trip in box ②, also trigger the alarm. An alarm means "worth checking," not "did something wrong." We never suspect a person from an alarm alone.

"High accuracy means it catches well"?

No. If anomalies are only 4%, answering "all normal" alone gives 96% accuracy. Look at recall and precision together with the number of alarms.

"A complex tool is always better"?

No. Isolation Forest won the showdown because the data had two clusters. With one cluster and anomalies at one end, as in box ①, a z-score measured with the median is enough, and it's easier to explain why.

The Doctor's thought cloud

Boiled down to one line, today is this: score with a ruler that fits the data's shape, draw the threshold at as many as you can look at, and have a person check.

Looking at the infographic's Python code (you don't need to know it)

Box 6 of the infographic runs scikit-learn's Isolation Forest in a few lines. The same content is shown here with explanations.[1][6]

Expand code
from sklearn.ensemble import IsolationForest

model = IsolationForest(
    contamination=0.05,   # sets the threshold so the top 5% of training scores are treated as anomalies
    random_state=42       # keeps the random cuts the same every run (for reproducing results)
)
labels = model.fit_predict(X)   # X: table of numbers (rows = records, columns = features)
# labels: -1 if anomaly, 1 if normal
scores = model.score_samples(X) # lower means more anomalous (the opposite sign of the original paper's score)
  • contamination is not a value that discovers the fact that "this data has 5% anomalies"; it's an instruction to put the threshold at the top 5% of the training data's scores. It's the same as the "top p%" method in box ⑤. Whether the real anomalies are 1% or 10%, about 5% of the training data gets −1. The default is "auto," which uses the original paper's criterion.[6]
  • score_samples is the original paper's score s(x) with its sign flipped, so "lower means more anomalous." The s(x) on this page is exactly the original paper's, where "closer to 1 means more anomalous." The infographic's Note also points out this difference.[1][6]
  • The default number of trees (n_estimators) is 100, and the default number of cases drawn per tree (max_samples) is the smaller of 256 and the number of data cases. This page uses the same values.[6]

Wrapping up the experiment

Doctor

Today we confirmed three things.

  • Anomaly detection has two steps: making the score and setting the threshold.
  • Choose the tool by the shape of the data. One column → Z; relationships → Mahalanobis; several clusters → Isolation Forest; patterns → reconstruction error.
  • The threshold is set by the staff available to check and the loss when a case is missed.
